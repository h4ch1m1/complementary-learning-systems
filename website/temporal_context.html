Introduction

Episodic memory lets us remember when something happened and what happened alongside it. A classic way to study this is free recall: participants study a list of words, then report the words they remember in any order. The order they choose reveals how memory organizes experiences in time.

Two patterns often appear in these experiments. Recall tends to begin near the end of the list, the recency effect. After one word is recalled, the next often comes from a nearby position, the contiguity effect. Among nearby words, the following word is more likely to be recalled than the preceding one; this forward bias is called asymmetry. After viewing the number cards below, for example, we might first recall the final card, 5. After recalling 6, we are more likely to recall the following card, 8, than the preceding card, 1. Experiences that occur close together in time tend to stay connected in memory.

Howard and Kahana (2002) proposed that timing is represented by a slowly changing state of temporal context. Each word becomes associated with the context in which it appears. At test, the current context serves as a retrieval cue and most closely resembles the contexts of the last few words. Recalling a word brings back part of its study context, making nearby words easier to recall.

To explain both effects, context must capture two temporal relationships. Recency depends on how much time has passed since each word appeared. Contiguity requires returning to the context of a recalled word and recovering how close other words were to it.

Earlier models treated context as a set of randomly changing elements. They could describe recency, but lacked a way to recover an earlier context and explain contiguity. In the temporal context model (TCM), the content of experience determines how context changes. Each word retrieves an associated context and blends it into the current state. Recalling the word retrieves that context again. A forward bias follows because the context introduced by a word persists only in subsequent states. With this mechanism and a small set of parameters, the authors sought to explain recency and contiguity over both short and long intervals.

Temporal context model

The temporal context model describes learning and recall through associations between words and context. During study, each word becomes linked to the context in which it appears. During recall, the current context activates words, and a recalled word changes the context in turn. We illustrate this process with a list of 12 words.

1. Items and temporal context

The model has an item layer F and a context layer T. The item layer represents the word currently being presented or recalled, with one unit per word: when “apple” is presented, its unit is active and all others are inactive. Activity in the context layer is represented by a vector t, which retains the influence of past experience and changes as new items arrive.

The words already have a history before the experiment. The model calls the context retrieved through these existing associations pre-experimental context. For a randomly assembled word list, different words are assumed to have unrelated pre-experimental contexts, represented here by orthogonal vectors.

2. Learning and context updating

When word i first appears, MFT retrieves its pre-experimental context tINi. This input combines with the previous context to form the new state:

ti = ρti−1 + βtINi

β controls the influence of new input on context. As distinct words are studied, a larger β produces faster context change and a faster decline in the influence of earlier inputs. ρ keeps the context vector at unit length; it is not an additional free parameter. When the input is orthogonal to the previous context, ρ = √(1 − β²).

General expression for ρ
ρi = √(1 + β²[(ti−1·tINi)² − 1]) − β(ti−1·tINi)

When the dot product is zero, the expression reduces to √(1 − β²). A repeated word can retrieve components that overlap with the current context, so ρ must then be calculated from their dot product.

After updating context, the model stores the association between the current word and this context in MTF. Because context changes gradually, neighboring words have similar study contexts. Words farther apart in the list have less similar contexts.

3. First recall

At the start of recall, the current context activates the words through MTF. When each word has been studied once, its activation is the dot product of its study context and the test context:

ai = ti · ttest

The study contexts of the last few words resemble the test context most closely, giving those words higher activation. The model converts activations into recall probabilities, with the denominator summing over all words in the list:

P(i) = exp(2ai/τ) / Σj exp(2aj/τ)

τ controls how strongly recall probabilities depend on differences in activation. A small τ concentrates probability on highly activated words; a larger τ makes the probabilities more similar.

A word’s recall probability depends on how its activation compares with that of other words. Distraction before test changes context, usually reducing these differences and weakening the advantage of recent words. Recency can persist as long as the final items retain higher activation.

4. Recall and context reinstatement

A recalled word retrieves context through MFT again. Learning has changed its associations, so the retrieved context now contains both its pre-experimental context and the context in which it appeared in this list. γ is the ratio of the former weight to the latter; the paper uses γ = 1, giving both components equal weight.

These components affect subsequent recall differently. The study context resembles the contexts of both preceding and following neighbors, supporting recall in both directions. The pre-experimental context entered the context state when the word appeared and persisted in later states, but was absent from earlier ones. This component favors later items and produces the forward bias.

5. Delay and continuous distraction

Howard and Kahana (1999) compared free recall under three timing conditions. Immediate recall begins as soon as the list ends. Delayed recall follows 16 seconds of arithmetic after the list. In continuous-distractor recall, arithmetic also follows each word, separating the words in time.

Arithmetic also changes context. The model represents a distractor interval by its effective length d: the number of unrelated items that would produce the same context change. The authors reasoned that similar arithmetic problems may produce less change than new words over the same duration. Thus, d is fitted to the data rather than measured directly in seconds.

The paper uses two measures of the temporal organization of recall. The probability of first recall describes where the first response appeared in the study list. The conditional response probability (CRP) describes transitions between recalled words, adjusted for the opportunities to make each transition. Their difference in study position is the lag: +1 means the next word in the list, and −1 means the preceding word. Here, CRP includes only the transition from the first to the second response.
